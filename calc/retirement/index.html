Social Security's retirement trust fund is projected to run out in Q4 2032 (2026 Trustees Report). After that, benefits can only be paid from incoming payroll and benefit taxes: about 78% of scheduled benefits at first, falling to about 60% by the 2080s as the number of retirees per worker grows.

When on, all benefits paid after Q4 2032 are reduced to the Trustees' projected payable share for each year. This assumes Congress never acts and that the shortfall is applied as an equal cut to everyone. Current law doesn't actually say how a shortfall would be handled.

How much faster national average wages grow than inflation. Only used for the Social Security estimate. Your earnings record and the benefit formula are indexed to national wages until you turn 60, so faster wage growth means a higher starting benefit.

The default of 1% sits between the Social Security Trustees' long-term projection (about 1.1%/yr) and the actual 1969–2019 average (about 0.8%/yr). Set it to 0% to match the "today's dollars" estimate on your SSA statement, which assumes no future wage growth.

How much market returns swing from year to year (standard deviation). Each simulated scenario draws a random return every year that averages out to your return assumption over time, so it includes crashes and booms at random times.

This matters most around retirement: a crash right after you start withdrawing hurts far more than one decades earlier. US stocks have swung about 20%/yr historically and high-quality bonds about 8%. The defaults assume a target-date-style mix: about 75% stocks on average before retirement (15%) and about 35% after (9%). Set to 0 to ignore market swings.
